# PLI Annotated Bibliography

**Purpose:** Source documentation for all PLI adjudication tracks: (a) the six-year (2026–2031) macroeconomic baseline and lever × indicator matrix (Sources 1–10, tagged **Macro**); (b) National Interest six-domain tier adjudication (Sources 11–14, tagged **National Interest**); (c) Glasl escalation staging (Sources 15–17, tagged **Escalation / Glasl**); (d) Diplomacy indexing taxonomy (Sources 18–20, tagged **Diplomacy**); and (e) Information SME briefing practice (Sources 21–22, tagged **Information**).

**Selection standard:** Institutional forecasters and peer-reviewed or statutory/central-bank research for Macro; National War College / NSS corpus and peer-reviewed interest-intensity literature for National Interest; Glasl’s conflict-escalation model and peer-reviewed conflict-management applications for Escalation; U.S. Department of State FAM/FAH materials plus scholarly public-diplomacy literature for Diplomacy; peer-reviewed information-environment literature for Information briefs (no numeric scoring).

**Model tags:** Each source header ends with the track it grounds, e.g. `(Macro)`, `(National Interest)`.

## Source 1 — IMF, United States 2026 Article IV Consultation (Country Report No. 26/76) `(Macro)`

**Citation:** International Monetary Fund. *United States: 2026 Article IV Consultation — Press Release; Staff Report.* IMF Country Report No. 26/76, March 17, 2026.
**URL:** https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/cr/2026/english/1usaea2026001.pdf

**What it provides:** The single most complete table for our purpose — "Selected Economic Indicators, 2020–31" — with annual projections for every PLI indicator through **2031**, exactly matching the six-year game horizon.

**Key projections (2026 → 2031):**
- Real GDP growth: 2.5 → 2.2 → 2.1 → 1.9 → 1.8 → 1.8
- PCE inflation (Q4/Q4): 2.8 → 2.0 → 2.0 → 2.0 → 2.0 → 2.0
- Unemployment rate: 4.3 → 4.2 → 4.0 → 3.9 → 3.9 → 3.9
- Goods export volume growth: 0.5 → 2.3 → 2.4 → 1.7 → 1.8 → 1.7
- Goods import volume growth: 1.4 → 4.8 → 0.3 → 1.0 → 1.5 → 1.3
- Private fixed investment growth: 4.0 → 3.3 → 2.1 → 1.8 → 1.8 → 1.8
- Current account balance: -3.8 to -3.6 % of GDP (context, not a scored indicator)

**Reliability:** IMF Article IV staff projections are produced under a published surveillance framework, reconciled against national accounts (BEA/BLS), and reviewed by the Executive Board. Historical forecast-error ranges are documented by the IMF's own Independent Evaluation Office.

**Use in PLI:** **Primary anchor for all five baseline trend lines.** It is the only source with all five indicators on a single consistent basis through 2031.

## Source 2 — IMF, World Economic Outlook (April 2026) `(Macro)`

**Citation:** International Monetary Fund. *World Economic Outlook: Global Economy in the Shadow of War.* April 14, 2026.
**URL:** https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/weo/2026/april/english/text.pdf

**What it provides:** Global context and cross-checks: world output growth 3.1% (2026) and 3.2% (2027); US growth 2.3% (2026) and 2.1% (2027); US public debt path to ~143% of GDP by 2031; trade-policy assumption that tariff measures in force at end-2025 are permanent.

**Reliability:** The WEO is the most widely used institutional forecast in existence; its database publishes vintages so forecast revisions are fully traceable.

**Use in PLI:** Corroborates the Article IV GDP path (WEO 2.3% vs Article IV 2.5% for 2026 — differences reflect Q4/Q4 vs annual-average bases). Supplies the standing **trade-policy assumption** that the baseline already embeds 2025-era tariffs — important so adjudicators do not double-count existing tariffs as a new action.

## Source 3 — Congressional Budget Office, The Budget and Economic Outlook: 2026 to 2036 (February 2026) `(Macro)`

**Citation:** Congressional Budget Office. *The Budget and Economic Outlook: 2026 to 2036.* February 11, 2026.
**URL:** https://www.crfb.org/papers/cbos-february-2026-budget-and-economic-outlook (summary); CBO publication.

**What it provides:** A ten-year legislated-policy baseline: real GDP growth 2.2% (2026) slowing to a 1.8% annual average; PCE inflation 2.7% (2026) returning to 2.0% **by 2030** (slower than the IMF's 2027); unemployment peaking at 4.6% (2026) and declining to 4.2% by 2032; CPI inflation 2.9% (2026), 2.3% ten-year average.

## Part B — Sources grounding the directionality matrix and Implementation modulation

The four sources below anchor the lever × indicator directionality matrix (which way each lever pushes each indicator, how hard, and with what onset) and the Implementation modulation table (why weakly implemented actions deliver diluted, delayed effects). They are findings about *direction, relative magnitude, and timing* — exactly the layer PLI adjudicates — not point forecasts.

## Source 7 — Amiti, Redding & Weinstein, "The Impact of the 2018 Tariffs on Prices and Welfare" (2019) `(Macro)`

**Citation:** Amiti, Mary, Stephen J. Redding, and David E. Weinstein. "The Impact of the 2018 Tariffs on Prices and Welfare." *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 33(4), Fall 2019, pp. 187–210. DOI: 10.1257/jep.33.4.187.

**What it provides:** The benchmark empirical study of the 2018 United States tariff waves. Finds **complete pass-through** of the tariffs into domestic prices of imported goods, with the full incidence falling on domestic consumers and importers; an aggregate United States real-income loss of roughly $1.4 billion per month by end-2018; roughly $165 billion per year of trade redirected to avoid the tariffs; and a ~1 percentage point rise in average United States manufacturing prices from combined input/output tariff effects.

**Reliability:** Published in a peer-reviewed American Economic Association journal; among the most-cited empirical papers on modern tariff policy (700+ citations); findings replicated in the parallel literature (Fajgelbaum et al. 2020, *Quarterly Journal of Economics*; Cavallo et al. 2021).

**Use in PLI:** Grounds the **L1 Trade & Customs matrix row**: inflation +M (complete pass-through into domestic prices), trade -S (large redirection and volume suppression), GDP -s (real-income loss), investment -s, and **short quarterly onset / fast ramp** (`onset_quarters` 0–1, `ramp_in_quarters` ≈ 2 — price effects appeared within months).

## Source 8 — Crosignani, Han, Macchiavelli & Silva, "Securing Technological Leadership? The Cost of Export Controls on Firms" (2024, rev. 2025) `(Macro)`

**Citation:** Crosignani, Matteo, Lina Han, Marco Macchiavelli, and André F. Silva. "Securing Technological Leadership? The Cost of Export Controls on Firms." Federal Reserve Bank of New York Staff Reports, No. 1096, April 2024, revised February 2025. DOI: 10.59576/sr.1096.

**What it provides:** The leading empirical study of United States export controls on China-linked supply chains. Finds that export controls trigger a **broad-based, involuntary decoupling**: affected United States suppliers terminate relations with both targeted and non-targeted Chinese customers (terminations up 50–75%), form 60–68% fewer new relationships in targeted countries, and show **no evidence of reshoring or friend-shoring within three years**. Affected suppliers lose roughly $130 billion in market capitalization and experience reduced profitability, employment, and bank lending.

**Reliability:** Federal Reserve Bank of New York staff research with published data and methodology; authors are Federal Reserve research economists.

**Use in PLI:** Grounds the **L2 Export Controls matrix row**: trade -M (broad decoupling), investment -s and GDP -tr (costs borne by the acting economy's own firms), onset ~2–4 quarters (terminations prompt; macro/reshoring lag). The "no reshoring within three years" finding supports scoring resilience claims conservatively and keeps friend-shoring ramps slow.

## Source 9 — Felbermayr, Kirilakha, Syropoulos, Yalcin & Yotov, "The Global Sanctions Data Base" (2020) `(Macro)`

**Citation:** Felbermayr, Gabriel, Aleksandra Kirilakha, Constantinos Syropoulos, Erdal Yalcin, and Yoto V. Yotov. "The Global Sanctions Data Base." *European Economic Review* 129, 2020, 103561. DOI: 10.1016/j.euroecorev.2020.103561.

**What it provides:** The most comprehensive dataset of economic sanctions (1950–2016; later releases extend to 2022), with structural-gravity estimates of their trade effects. Core finding: **trade sanctions have a negative but heterogeneous effect on trade — most pronounced for complete bilateral sanctions, followed by complete export sanctions**, while partial sanctions show smaller and sometimes insignificant aggregate effects. Documents the shift toward financial (rather than trade) sanctions and declining success rates since 1995.

**Reliability:** Peer-reviewed (*European Economic Review*); the dataset is the standard reference in the quantitative sanctions literature and is publicly maintained and updated.

**Use in PLI:** Twofold. (1) Grounds the **L4 Financial Sanctions matrix row**: trade -M, with small price and output costs to the acting economy. (2) Grounds the **Implementation magnitude-downgrade ladder** and slower/weaker quarterly weights under partial execution: complete sanctions bite hardest; partial ones do not deliver instant full bite.

## Source 10 — Congressional Budget Office, federal investment analyses (2016, 2021) `(Macro)`

**Citation:** Congressional Budget Office. *The Macroeconomic and Budgetary Effects of Federal Investment.* June 2016 (Publication 51628). — and — *Effects of Physical Infrastructure Spending on the Economy and the Budget Under Two Illustrative Scenarios.* August 2021 (Publication 57407). See also CBO, "Estimating the Long-Term Effects of Federal R&D Spending" (2018, Publication 54089).

**What it provides:** CBO's standing framework for how public investment reaches the economy. Key findings: federal investment raises private-sector productivity and output **only gradually**; of an illustrative $500 billion infrastructure authorization, only about **$12 billion is actually spent in the first year** (roughly $33 billion by year two, 96% only by 2046); macroeconomic effects of applied research spending begin only after roughly ten years; and the net GDP effect depends on financing (borrowing-financed investment yields roughly half the ten-year GDP gain of offset-financed investment).

**Reliability:** CBO is the statutorily nonpartisan scoring agency of the United States Congress; its methods and forecast-error evaluations are published, and these reports are the reference framework used in actual legislative scoring.

**Use in PLI:** Grounds three design choices. (1) The **L7 Industrial Policy matrix row**: strongest positive investment impulse (+S) but long `onset_quarters` (~8) and multi-year `ramp_in_quarters` (~12 for GDP). (2) The **L6 Development Finance** long onset/ramp. (3) The **Implementation onset-delay column** in quarters (+4 / +8): the documented multi-year gap between authorization, outlay, and macroeconomic effect — and against CBO's ten-year horizon for research spending, the game's delays remain conservative.

## Quarterly adjudication grid and month anchors

**Codebook version:** `trial-2026-07-13-quarterly`.

PLI adjudicates on a **quarterly grid** (`2026Q1` … `2034Q4`), not annual bricks. FO 2.0 worksheets carry a required `submission_month` (`YYYY-MM`); the engine maps month → calendar quarter and measures onset, Implementation delay, ramp-in, plateau, and decay in quarters:

`start_quarter = submission_quarter + onset_quarters + impl_delay_quarters`

`delta[q] = sign × magnitude × weight(q)` with `weight ∈ [0,1]` from ramp → plateau → decay (bib-cited profile fields on each lever×indicator cell).

| Timing behavior | Bib anchor | How it enters PLI |
|---|---|---|
| Coercive trade/price effects within months | Source 7 | L1: short onset, fast ramp |
| Export-control decoupling prompt; reshoring lag | Source 8 | L2: onset ~2–4Q; slow resilience claims |
| Sanctions completeness → magnitude | Source 9 | L4 + Implementation downgrades; partial → weaker weights |
| Public investment gradual outlay/productivity | Source 10 | L6/L7: long onset + multi-year ramp; Impl delay adds quarters |
| Baseline path / horizon | Sources 1–6 | Annual institutional rates expanded by **within-year hold** onto the quarterly grid through 2034Q4 |

**Discretization rule (Sources 1–6):** Institutions publish annual or Q4/Q4 rates. The quarterly series holds each year's institutional rate constant across Q1–Q4 of that year. This is an adjudication discretization for month anchors and path shape — **not** a claim of higher-frequency institutional point forecasts. Fed SEP inflation already frames Q4/Q4; annual identity is preserved when aggregating quarters for display if needed.

**Multi-action stacking (game rule, not a bib lag):** How multiple actions combine on the same indicator-quarter (`uncapped` default, or `same_quarter` / `per_move` clamps at ±S) is a FO 2.0 display/adjudication policy in the codebook and engine. It does **not** introduce a new lag length and does not require a Sources 7–10 timing update.

**Rule:** no new lag length without a bibliography “Use in PLI” update and a codebook changelog row.

## Synthesis — the adopted baseline (2026–2031, held through 2034Q4)

Primary anchor: IMF Article IV (Source 1). Convergence tail checked against Fed SEP (Source 4). Trade corroborated by WTO (Source 5). Inflation normalization window bracketed by CBO (slow bound, Source 3) and OECD (shock bound, Source 6).

| Indicator | 2026 | 2027 | 2028 | 2029 | 2030 | 2031 | Directionality summary |
|-----------|------|------|------|------|------|------|------------------------|
| Real GDP growth (%) | 2.5 | 2.2 | 2.1 | 1.9 | 1.8 | 1.8 | Gradual glide to ~1.8% potential |
| PCE inflation (Q4/Q4, %) | 2.8 | 2.0 | 2.0 | 2.0 | 2.0 | 2.0 | Normalizes to target by 2027 (CBO slow bound: 2030) |
| Unemployment rate (%) | 4.3 | 4.2 | 4.0 | 3.9 | 3.9 | 3.9 | Slight improvement, then flat near 4% |
| Trade volume growth (goods, %) | 1.0 | 3.6 | 1.4 | 1.4 | 1.7 | 1.5 | Subdued with 2027 rebound; ±0.5 pp WTO band |
| Fixed investment growth (private, %) | 4.0 | 3.3 | 2.1 | 1.8 | 1.8 | 1.8 | Strong AI-driven start, normalizing |

**Known disagreements (flagged for SME review):**
1. **Inflation normalization date** — IMF/Fed say 2027; CBO says 2030 (tariff persistence); OECD says a 2026 spike to 4.2% then undershoot to 1.6%. The trend line uses the IMF path; the chart carries a shaded band spanning CBO and OECD bounds.
2. **2026 GDP** — range of 2.0 (OECD) to 2.5 (IMF Article IV annual). Direction is identical (deceleration toward 1.8); magnitude differences do not affect directionality adjudication.
3. **Trade 2027 rebound** — IMF import-volume rebound (4.8%) is larger than WTO world figure (2.6%); both agree on direction.

**Why directionality, not point estimates:** Across these six institutions, six-year *point* forecasts differ by up to 1.4 pp on the same indicator in the same year, but *direction and shape* (decelerating growth, normalizing inflation, flat unemployment, subdued trade, front-loaded investment) are unanimous. PLI therefore adjudicates against direction and shape — the empirically defensible layer of the forecast.

## Part D — Escalation / Glasl `(Escalation / Glasl)`

## Source 15 — Glasl, *Confronting Conflict* / conflict-escalation model (stages 1–9) `(Escalation / Glasl)`

**Citation:** Glasl, Friedrich. *Confronting Conflict: A First-Aid Kit for Handling Conflict.* Stroud: Hawthorn Press, 1999 (English ed.; German originals earlier). Model also summarized in Glasl, “Konfliktmanagement” practitioner literature.

**Use in PLI:** Primary stage definitions for the Escalation track (`05_GLASL_ESCALATION_CODEBOOK.md`).

## Source 16 — Glasl, "Konfliktmanagement" stage operationalization in peer conflict-resolution literature `(Escalation / Glasl)`

**Citation:** Glasl, Friedrich. *Konfliktmanagement: Ein Handbuch für Führungskräfte, Beraterinnen und Berater.* Bern: Haupt Verlag (multiple editions; English operational summaries in conflict-resolution curricula). **Reliability:** Glasl’s handbook is the primary practitioner source for the nine-stage model; Pruitt & Kim is a standard peer-reviewed/academic conflict-resolution text that situates escalation as a structured process rather than a vibe judgment.

**Use in PLI:** Clarifies stage-matching criteria adjudicators cite when proposing a per-action Δ, alongside Source 15’s primary Glasl definitions.
